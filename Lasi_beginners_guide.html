A Beginner’s Guide to Working with LASI Data

Notes for students getting started with the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI): how to request and download the data, how the survey design and codebooks are organised, and the first steps in Stata or R. Written as a walk-through rather than a formal course. It is a living document — sections, figures, and worked examples will be filled in as I go.

LASI Data access Survey design Stata R Last updated: 2026

1.What is LASI?

LASI is India’s first nationally representative longitudinal survey of adults aged 45 and above, designed to mirror the family of Health and Retirement Studies (HRS, SHARE, CHARLS, etc.). It is the main source for studying ageing, health, and economic wellbeing in India, and it links naturally to the questions I work on around elderly wellbeing and household structure.

This section will eventually include: waves and coverage, sampling frame, key modules, and how LASI compares to NFHS and NSSO for the kinds of questions you might want to ask.

2.Getting the data

LASI is not a public download the way NFHS is. Access is managed by the IIPS / LASI team and requires a short application. The exact steps below are the ones I followed — they may change.

2.1 Requesting access

Identify yourself and your institution
You will need an institutional email and a supervisor or PI who can vouch for the use of the data.
Submit the data request form
Briefly state the research question, the variables you need, and the level of geography (state / district / individual).
Sign the data use agreement
Standard confidentiality terms — no re-distribution, no attempt to re-identify respondents.
Receive the download link
Typically a secure link plus a codebook and a short README. Keep the README — it saves hours later.

3.Survey structure

3.1 Design and weights

LASI uses a multistage stratified sampling design. For any descriptive estimate you need to account for the sampling weights and the PSU/strata structure; ignoring them will give you point estimates and confidence intervals that are wrong in ways that are easy to miss.

This subsection will eventually show: how to identify the weight variable, how to declare the survey design in Stata (svyset) and R (survey / srvyr), and a worked example of a weighted proportion with a design-correct standard error.

3.2 Reading the codebook

The codebook is where most beginner mistakes are avoided. Look for three things on every variable before you use it:

  • Value labels. Codes like 9, 99, -8 usually mean “don’t know / refused / not applicable” and must be set to missing before any analysis.
  • Skip logic. Many questions are only asked of a subset (e.g. only women, only those reporting a condition). The denominator matters.
  • Wave. A variable that exists in Wave 1 may be dropped or renamed in Wave 2.

4.First steps in Stata / R

The snippets below are the minimum needed to get a clean, weighted descriptive estimate. They are placeholders for the worked examples that will grow here.

4.1 Stata

* Load the individual module use "lasi_individual_w1.dta", clear * Recode special missing values recode var1 (9 99 -8 = .) * Declare the survey design svyset psu [pw = weight_var], strata(strata_var) * Weighted proportion with design-correct SE svy: proportion var1

4.2 R

library(survey) library(srvyr) lasi <- haven::read_dta("lasi_individual_w1.dta") lasi <- lasi |> mutate(var1 = na_if(var1, 9), var1 = na_if(var1, 99)) des <- lasi |> as_survey_design(ids = psu, strata = strata_var, weights = weight_var) des |> summarise(prop = survey_mean(var1, na.rm = TRUE))

5.Weights & variance

Two practical rules I keep coming back to: (i) always report the design you used, and (ii) never compare a weighted estimate from LASI to an unweighted estimate from another survey and assume the difference is substantive. This section will spell out the design variables, the difference between analytic and post-stratified weights, and a checklist for reproducible weighted estimates.

6.Common pitfalls

  • Forgetting to set special codes (9 / 99 / -8) to missing.
  • Analysing without svyset and reporting inflated precision.
  • Merging on the wrong identifier — check that the merge is 1:1 or 1:m as documented.
  • Treating a variable that is only asked of a subset as if it were asked of everyone.
  • Comparing variables across waves without checking the codebook for renames.
